# The Overnight Return Anomaly: A Literature Review

This project's core question, is "buy the close, sell the open" a real, documented market phenomenon or a chart trick, turns out to be a 40-year-old academic literature, not a novel discovery. This note summarizes that literature in roughly chronological order, so a reader can see where the idea came from, how it's evolved, and what's still unsettled. See [`../report.md`](../report.md) for this project's own empirical test of it.

## The idea in one paragraph

Every trading day's return can be split into two pieces: what happens while the market is *closed* (previous close to today's open, "overnight") and what happens while it's *open* (today's open to close, "intraday"). Naively you'd expect both to look similar. They don't: across long samples of US stocks, the overnight piece has historically captured a disproportionate share, sometimes nearly all, of the total return, while the intraday piece is flat, noisy, or even negative on average. Forty years of finance research has tried to document how general this is, and why it happens.

---

## 1. The discovery: trading hours and non-trading hours are fundamentally different

**French, K. R., & Roll, R. (1986). "Stock Return Variances: The Arrival of Information and the Reaction of Traders."** *Journal of Financial Economics*, 17(1), 5-26.
[ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0304405X86900048) · [Free PDF (U. Toronto)](http://www-2.rotman.utoronto.ca/~kan/3032/pdf/AssetPricingAnomalies/French_Roll_JFE_1986.pdf)

The paper that started this line of research, though it's about *volatility*, not returns. French and Roll found that stock return variance per hour is dramatically higher during exchange trading hours than during weekends/holidays of equivalent elapsed time: trading itself, not just the passage of time, generates volatility. They test three explanations (public information arrival, private/informed trading, and noise from mispricing) and conclude private information and trading-induced mispricing both matter. This established that trading-hour and non-trading-hour returns are generated by different processes, the premise everything below builds on.

## 2. The empirical extension: almost the entire equity premium is earned overnight

**Cooper, M. J., Cliff, M. T., & Gulen, H. (2008). "Return Differences between Trading and Non-Trading Hours: Like Night and Day."** Working paper (later published as "Returns in Trading versus Non-Trading Hours: The Difference is Day and Night," *Journal of Asset Management*, 2011).
[SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1004081) · [Journal of Asset Management](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/jam.2011.2)

Using transaction-level data across individual NYSE/Nasdaq stocks, equity indices, and index futures, the authors show the day/night split isn't a curiosity of one or two names: it holds broadly, and it's *consistent* across days of the week, days of the month, and months of the year. Their headline finding: essentially all of the long-run US equity premium is earned overnight; average intraday (open-to-close) returns are close to zero and sometimes negative. This is the paper that turns the MU-chart-style claim from "look at this one weird stock" into "this is apparently how the whole market works."

## 3. The leading behavioral mechanism: retail investors buying at the open

**Berkman, H., Koch, P. D., Tuttle, L., & Zhang, Y. J. (2012). "Paying Attention: Overnight Returns and the Hidden Cost of Buying at the Open."** *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis*, 47(4), 715-741.
[Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/paying-attention-overnight-returns-and-the-hidden-cost-of-buying-at-the-open/F9AAD159B512C651F09D5D52011D88E0) · [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228292307_Paying_Attention_Overnight_Returns_and_the_Hidden_Cost_of_Buying_at_the_Open)

The most-cited explanation for *why* the overnight effect exists. Retail investors can't watch markets all day, so when a stock catches their attention (a big move, news, high trading volume), the market open is their first chance to act on it, and they buy. That buying pressure shows up as a price pop at the open, which then partially reverses during the day as institutional/smart money fades the flow. Consistent with this, the effect is strongest in stocks that are hard to value or arbitrage, and during high-sentiment periods. This is also this project's own [§3 finding](../report.md#3-where-the-effect-concentrates-growth-attention-sectors-not-the-whole-market): the effect concentrates in high-attention growth/tech/consumer stocks and is absent or reversed in boring staples/utilities names that don't attract that kind of speculative morning flow.

## 4. The modern definitive paper: overnight and intraday are two different return series

**Lou, D., Polk, C., & Skouras, S. (2019). "A Tug of War: Overnight Versus Intraday Expected Returns."** *Journal of Financial Economics*, 134(1), 192-213.
[ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X19300650) · [Free working paper (LSE)](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/polk/research/TugOfWar.pdf)

Shows overnight and intraday returns behave almost like two separate return series with *opposite* momentum/reversal properties: a stock's past overnight returns predict its *future* overnight returns (momentum), while its past intraday returns predict *future* intraday reversals. The authors tie this to investor clienteles: institutional ownership rises more with intraday returns than overnight returns, implying different investor types dominate each session and pull prices in different directions. This project's [§4 factor regression](../report.md#4-is-this-just-repackaged-momentum) is a direct empirical check on this paper's own momentum framing: it finds the *aggregate market momentum factor* isn't what's driving the effect in our universe (the loading is statistically zero), even though momentum is clearly present in the overnight-return-predicts-overnight-return sense Lou/Polk/Skouras document at the stock level. Those are related but distinct claims, and it's worth not conflating them.

## 5. A market-microstructure theory: fast vs. slow liquidity providers

**Lu, Z., Malliaris, S. G., & Qin, Z. (2023). "Heterogeneous Liquidity Providers and Night-Minus-Day Return Predictability."** *Journal of Financial Economics*, 148(3), 175-200.
[ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X23000491) · [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4004609)

A more theoretical follow-up asking *why* characteristic-sorted portfolios often earn opposite-signed overnight and intraday returns. Builds a model with two types of arbitrageurs ("fast" ones with an informational edge and "slow" ones with low inventory-holding costs) competing to provide liquidity, and shows this structure alone can generate the night/day return split without needing a specific behavioral story about retail attention. Useful as a reminder that Berkman et al.'s mechanism isn't the only theoretical account that fits the data.

## 6. Where exactly does it happen? Narrowing the window with 24-hour futures

**Bondarenko, O., & Muravyev, D. (2022). "Market Return Around the Clock: A Puzzle."** *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis*, 58(3).
[Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/market-return-around-the-clock-a-puzzle/089E33AC0B4D3B9A02CBA31EDF6505B3) · [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3596245)

Using E-mini S&P 500 futures, which trade nearly continuously (so there's no single "close" and "open" the way there is for stocks), the authors find something sharper than "overnight is good, intraday is bad": essentially the *entire* average market return over decades is earned in a roughly 4-hour window around the European market open, with a Sharpe ratio around 1.6 that survives transaction costs. The other ~20 hours of the trading day average out to noisy zero. Their explanation: this is when European investors process information that accumulated overnight and uncertainty (VIX futures) resolves, consistent with an information-arrival story rather than a pure retail-attention one. This is a useful complication for anyone thinking about actually trading the effect: "overnight" as commonly measured (stock close to stock open) is a coarse proxy for a much narrower window where the actual return seems to concentrate.

## 7. The newest entry: does actual news content explain it?

**Glasserman, P., Krstovski, K., Laliberte, P., & Mamaysky, H. (2025). "Does Overnight News Explain Overnight Returns?"**
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.04481) · [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5336382)

The most recent and most direct attempt to explain the effect with actual information content rather than investor behavior alone. Using 2.4 million news articles and a supervised topic-modeling technique that selects news topics by their ability to explain contemporaneous returns, the authors find that time-variation in *which* news topics appear, and *how differently the market reacts* to news depending on when it breaks, together explain a substantial part of the intraday/overnight return gap. Their model forecasts out-of-sample which stocks will do well overnight and poorly intraday, evidence the effect has real informational content, not just a behavioral quirk of retail order flow. This is a live research question as of 2025, not a settled one.

## What this project adds

The papers above mostly establish the aggregate phenomenon and propose mechanisms. This project's contribution (see [`../report.md`](../report.md)) is narrower and more applied: taking one viral chart's claim about a single stock (Micron) and testing, with a sector-diversified 33-ticker universe and Newey-West HAC-robust statistics, (a) whether MU's *specific* pattern generalizes, (b) where the broader effect concentrates, (c) whether it survives controlling for the market/size/value/momentum factors the literature above discusses, and (d) whether it survives realistic transaction costs. Short answer: the aggregate effect is real and matches this literature; MU's extreme version of it does not generalize to most stocks.
